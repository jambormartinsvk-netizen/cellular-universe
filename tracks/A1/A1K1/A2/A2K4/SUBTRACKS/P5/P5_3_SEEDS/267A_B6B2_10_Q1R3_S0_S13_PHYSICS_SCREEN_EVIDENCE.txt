L14@P0: Abstract. The kinetic energy of the fluid shell in the cosmological first-order phase transition is crucial for predicting the gravitational wave signals generated by the sound wave
L15@P0: mechanism. We propose a model-dependent method to calculate the kinetic energy fraction
L16@P0: by dividing the bubble-fluid system into three distinct regions: the symmetric phase, the
L17@P0: broken phase, and the bubble wall. By solving the local equation of motion of the scalar
L18@P0: field with a phenomenological friction term, the bubble wall velocity and the boundary conditions of the fluid equations of both phases can be derived simultaneously. Then, for a
L19@P0: given particle physics model, the fluid profiles of different hydrodynamical modes and the
L20@P0: corresponding kinetic energy fraction can be obtained. Our method can also capture the
L21@P0: temperature dependency of the sound speed of the plasma. Compared with the conventional
L22@P0: model-independent method, our approach is based on an accurate equation of state derived
L23@P0: directly from the effective potential and takes into account the contribution of the bubble wall
L24@P0: to the energy-momentum tensor. Therefore, our method in-principle provides a more consistent and accurate result, which is crucial for high-precision calculations of the gravitational
L25@P0: waves induced by the first-order phase transition.

L43@P1: 1 Introduction
L44@P1: The cosmological first-order phase transition (FOPT) and its associated phase transition
L45@P1: gravitational waves (GWs) open a new window to explore many fundamental problems in
L46@P1: particle cosmology, such as electroweak baryogenesis [1, 2] and dark matter [3–7], etc. During
L47@P1: an FOPT, GW signals can be generated by bubble collisions [8–10], sound waves [11–13], and
L48@P1: turbulence [14–16]. Future GW experiments, such as TianQin [17, 18], LISA [19], Taiji [20],
L49@P1: and Big Bang Observatory (BBO) [21], among others, may be able to detect phase transition
L50@P1: GW signals and reveal various puzzles about our Universe. Recent studies [11–13] have shown
L51@P1: that the sound wave is the dominant source of phase transition GWs in a thermal FOPT. To
L52@P1: finally pin down the underlying physics of an FOPT, we need precise quantification of the
L53@P1: GW spectra and their signal-to-noise ratio in future GW experiments. To that end, obtaining
L54@P1: accurate estimations of phase transition parameters that determine the GW spectra become
L55@P1: critical. The two most crucial variables among these parameters are the bubble wall velocity
L56@P1: and the energy budget, where the latter is also known as the kinetic energy fraction, which
L57@P1: characterizes the fraction of energy released during an FOPT that is transferred into the
L58@P1: kinetic energy of the ambient fluid.
L59@P1: Previous studies [22–28] on the energy budget of an FOPT are based on the conventional
L60@P1: model-independent method. This method matches a well-motivated particle physics model
L61@P1: to a specific model of equation of state (EoS) according to some phase transition parameters,
L62@P1: such as the strength parameter and the bubble wall velocity. As a result, the boundary
L63@P1: conditions of the fluid equations can be determined, and then the corresponding kinetic
L64@P1: energy fraction can be derived. However, the specific model of EoS, such as the bag model,
L65@P1-2: – 1 –cannot fully capture the features of the symmetric and broken phases since some important
L66@P2: factors are ignored, such as the variation of the sound speed of the plasma. To address these
L67@P2: limitations, some other models of EoS are constructed. For example, the µν model [24–27] can
L68@P2: take the variation of the sound speed into account by considering it as an extra parameter.
L69@P2: Additionally, to incorporate the temperature-dependent sound speed, a more complicated
L70@P2: model of EoS is built in ref. [28], which includes more extra parameters. Although the modelindependent method is convenient for studying various particle physics models, the accuracy
L71@P2: of this method depends on the constructed EoS model. To capture all relevant properties, a
L72@P2: complicated EoS model should be considered, and sophisticated calculations could be involved.
L73@P2: Furthermore, the calculation of the bubble wall velocity is decoupled from the kinetic energy
L74@P2: fraction in the conventional model-independent method.
L75@P2: The bubble wall velocity is strongly correlated with the energy budget, and recent studies [29–32] show that the fluid dynamics can have a significant impact on it. To this end, in
L76@P2: this work, we unify the calculation of the energy budget and the bubble wall velocity into a
L77@P2: single framework. Given that the phase transition system is a coupled fluid-scalar system, we
L78@P2: divide it into three regions, which are the symmetric phase, the broken phase, and the bubble
L79@P2: wall. The plasma in the symmetric and broken phases is treated as a perfect fluid, and the
L80@P2: corresponding EoS of both phases are directly obtained from the effective potential of the
L81@P2: particle physics model, which is actually the free energy of the phase transition system. In
L82@P2: contrast to the conventional model-independent method, our approach does not require the
L83@P2: construction of a model of EoS but derives it directly from the effective potential. To obtain
L84@P2: the kinetic energy fraction, the fluid equations in both phases with proper boundary conditions need to be solved. In our framework, we derive the corresponding boundary conditions
L85@P2: by solving the local equations of motion (EoM) of the scalar field and the fluid across the
L86@P2: bubble wall. To simplify the calculation, a phenomenological friction term characterizing the
L87@P2: out-of-equilibrium phenomena is introduced. With the planar approximation, we solve the
L88@P2: scalar field and fluid profiles, thereby obtaining the bubble wall velocity and the boundary
L89@P2: conditions for the fluid equations of both phases. This allows us to finally solve the fluid
L90@P2: profiles in both phases and derive the energy budget. Since the contribution of the scalar
L91@P2: field to the energy-momentum tensor is taken into account, and the EoS is directly obtained
L92@P2: from the effective potential, our model-dependent method can provide more consistent and
L93@P2: accurate results in principle.
L94@P2: This work is organized as follow. In section 2, we introduce the general hydrodynamical
L95@P2: treatment of the FOPT system. Then we propose our model-dependent method for the
L96@P2: calculation of energy budget in section 3, where the definition of the kinetic energy fraction
L97@P2: and a review of the conventional model-independent method to quantify it are also given.
L98@P2: In section 4, we use a representative model to demonstrate our model-dependent analysis
L99@P2: method. Discussion and conclusion are given in section 5 and section 6, respectively.

L435@P6: 3 Kinetic energy fraction
L436@P6: For a thermal cosmological FOPT, the latent heat released by the phase transition must be
L437@P6: transferred into the gradient energy of the bubble wall, as well as the thermal energy and
